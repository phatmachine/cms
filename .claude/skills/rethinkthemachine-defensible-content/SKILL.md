# Defensible content

This platform tells people the truth about companies that can afford lawyers.
The job of this skill is to let it keep doing that — **not** to make the writing
timid. A page that says nothing protects no one and helps no one.

The stance, stated once, because every rule below serves it:

> We are telling the truth and facts matter. We also have a strong opinion, and
> that is allowed. We are advising people, and that advice comes from our view
> and our goal to educate.

All three parts of that are legally defensible positions. Truth is a complete
defence in all five markets. Honest opinion is protected in all five — *on
conditions*. Educating the public is the core of every public-interest defence
there is. What is not defensible is a fact we cannot evidence, an opinion with
no visible basis, or a commercial interest we did not disclose.

So the discipline is not "say less". It is **say it in the form that holds**.

**This is not legal advice and the author is not counsel.** It is a working
discipline for a writer. See "When to stop and get a lawyer" at the end — that
section is not decorative.

## The three tests, applied while writing

Run these on every sentence that names a company. They take seconds once they
are habit, and they are ordered by how expensive the mistake is.

### 1. Fact or opinion — and does the reader know which?

Fact can be false. Opinion, honestly held and resting on disclosed facts, is
protected everywhere in scope.

The trap is not opinion. The trap is **opinion that implies undisclosed facts**.
"In my view they sell your data" is treated as the factual claim, not as
opinion, because it implies I know something the reader does not.

The fix is structural, and it is the house style for this platform:

> **Sentence one: the fact, sourced, impersonal.
> Sentence two: what I make of it, in first person.**

```
Since August 2025 YouTube has used a model to estimate whether American users
are under eighteen. I am not willing to hand over more data to correct a guess.
```

The first sentence is checkable and true. The second is unmistakably mine and
rests on the first. That structure is why the guides moved to first person —
see `reason.NN.body` in any exit guide.

### 2. Can we prove it with what we hold?

Not "is it true" — *can we evidence it today, from the ledger?* A truth you
cannot produce on demand behaves like a falsehood when a letter arrives.

The rule from `rtm-exit-guide`: **a claim with no ledger row does not go on the
page.** This skill adds one clause — the row has to actually support the
sentence as printed. A row that says "reported by three affiliate directories,
figures conflict" does not support "they pay 64%".

Source strength, in order:
1. The company's own words — policy, filing, press release, executive on record
2. A regulator's finding, a court judgment, a published contract
3. Named, dated reporting from an outlet with a corrections policy
4. Everything else — **not printable**, however plausible

Attributed statistics deserve special care. They are the most checkable sentence
on any page, and a wrong one attributed to a named institution is the easiest
thing in the world for a critic to discredit. If the number cannot be traced to
the named body, either find the real figure or drop to a qualitative claim that
the real figure comfortably supports. (Worked example: the "92% of Western data"
line in `sources/sources-shared-claims.md`, which became "the majority".)

### 3. Who can sue, and where?

Jurisdiction changes the answer more than writers expect. The short version for
the five markets is below; the detail is in `references/jurisdictions.md`.

| Market | Can a big company sue for defamation? | The real risk here |
|---|---|---|
| **New Zealand** | Yes, but a body corporate must plead and prove pecuniary loss. No serious-harm threshold. | Home jurisdiction — this is where a letter actually lands. Fair Trading Act once we are trading. |
| **Australia** | **Mostly no.** Corporations with 10+ employees generally cannot sue for defamation. | Injurious falsehood instead — needs malice. Australian Consumer Law s.18. |
| **United Kingdom** | Yes, but must show serious *financial* loss. | Honest opinion requires the basis be indicated. ASA/CAP once commercial. |
| **European Union** | Member-state law; some states retain criminal defamation. | Comparative advertising rules; misleading omissions. |
| **United States** | Yes, but plaintiff proves falsity, and large companies are usually public figures needing actual malice. | FTC endorsement disclosure. Lanham Act if we compete commercially. |

Two things fall out of that table and are worth internalising:

- **The most permissive markets for criticism are the ones where our commercial
  behaviour is most regulated.** Australia will barely let Amazon sue us for
  defamation, and will happily let the ACCC look at an undisclosed affiliate
  link. As this platform monetises, the centre of gravity moves from defamation
  to consumer law. See `references/commercial-speech.md`.
- **New Zealand is where we live and where we get served.** It has no
  serious-harm filter, so the practical threshold is lower than the UK's.

## Constructions that draw fire

`rtm-leave-validation/references/legal.md` holds the full table of risky
constructions and their defensible rewrites — **read it rather than
reinventing it.** The pattern underneath all of them:

> **State the conduct. Name the source. Let the reader draw the conclusion.**

That is simultaneously the safest form and the more damning one, which is the
happy fact this whole discipline rests on. "They spy on you" is an accusation a
reader discounts. "Their own policy says human reviewers read what you type, and
asks you not to type anything you would not want read" is a fact a reader
repeats.

Four that recur in this platform's subject matter and are worth memorising:

| Do not write | Write |
|---|---|
| "This is illegal." | "[Regulator] fined them €X in [year], finding [finding]." |
| "They sell your data." | "Their policy permits sharing with [named categories]." |
| "They hand data to the government." | "They are subject to [named statute], which can compel disclosure without notice." |
| "Completely private." | "[Specific thing] is end-to-end encrypted, which means the provider cannot read it. Metadata is not." |

The last one is not defamation risk — it is consumer-law risk pointed at *us*,
for a promise about somebody else's product that we cannot keep.

## Strong opinion, safely

Opinion is where this platform's voice lives, so getting it right matters more
than avoiding it. Four conditions make an opinion defensible across all five
markets:

1. **It is recognisable as opinion.** First person does most of this work. "I am
   not willing to", "I would rather not", "I read that as a pattern".
2. **Its basis is visible.** The fact it rests on is on the same page, ideally
   the sentence before. This is an explicit statutory requirement in the UK and
   in substance the position elsewhere — and it is the single strongest argument
   for publishing sources rather than keeping them in a private ledger.
3. **An honest person could hold it** on those facts. Not "is it fair" — could
   someone reasonable land here.
4. **It is genuinely held.** Write nothing for effect that we do not believe.

What breaks it: opinion about **motive** ("they designed it to trap children"),
opinion about **named individuals' character**, and opinion phrased as though it
were reporting. Criticise companies, documented decisions and mechanisms.

## Before publishing anything that names a company

A short gate. If any answer is no, fix it before it ships.

- [ ] Every factual claim has a ledger row that supports the sentence **as printed**
- [ ] Every attributed statistic traces to the body it is attributed to
- [ ] Every opinion is first person and sits next to its factual basis
- [ ] No imputation of crime, no superlative, no absolute, no motive
- [ ] No promise about an alternative's security or privacy that we cannot evidence
- [ ] Every commercial relationship is disclosed near the recommendation, not in a footer
- [ ] The mandatory catch is intact and has not been softened to protect a commission
- [ ] Sources are archived, not just linked — a dead citation reads as an invented one
- [ ] No logos, wordmarks or app icons; naming only

## When to stop and get a lawyer

Escalate rather than rewrite when any of these is true. Rewriting around a
genuinely serious allegation does not dispose of it; it just hides it from us.

- The claim alleges **criminal conduct** by a company or a person
- The claim rests on **leaked or discovery material**
- The claim is about a **named individual** rather than a company
- A company has **already written to us** about anything
- The page makes claims a reader will **act on financially**
- We are asserting a company acted **for or with a government or agency** and
  the evidence is not a published contract, named statute, court ruling,
  regulator finding, transparency report or on-record statement

One session with a New Zealand media lawyer is worth more than any amount of
careful self-editing, and becomes more worthwhile — not less — as the platform
starts earning commission, because commercial speech narrows the latitude that
editorial criticism enjoys.
