# Jurisdictions — the five markets

Australia and New Zealand in detail, because `rtm-leave-validation/references/legal.md`
covers the UK, EU and US and does not cover these two at all. New Zealand
matters most: it is where this platform is published from and where a letter
would actually arrive.

**Not legal advice.** A working reference for a writer, and a snapshot that will
drift. Verify anything load-bearing before it carries weight, and route serious
allegations to a qualified lawyer in the relevant market.

---

## The comparison, in one table

| | NZ | AU | UK | EU | US |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Big company can sue for defamation | Yes, with pecuniary loss | **Generally no** | Yes, with serious financial loss | Member-state law | Yes, but actual malice |
| Threshold filter | Pecuniary loss for corporates; **no serious-harm test** | Serious harm + corporate exclusion | Serious harm (financial, for traders) | Varies | Actual malice for public figures |
| Who proves truth/falsity | Defendant proves truth | Defendant proves truth | Defendant proves truth | Varies | **Plaintiff** proves falsity |
| Corporate workaround | — | Injurious falsehood (needs malice) | Malicious falsehood | Varies | Trade libel |
| Consumer-law exposure once trading | Fair Trading Act 1986 | Australian Consumer Law s.18 | DMCCA 2024 / CAP Code | Dir. 2005/29/EC | FTC Endorsement Guides |
| Criminal defamation | No | No | No | **Some states** | No |

The pattern worth holding in your head: **the markets that most protect our
criticism are the ones that most regulate our commerce.** Australia will barely
let a large company sue us for what we say about it, and will take a keen
interest in whether we disclosed an affiliate link. As this platform earns
commission, the risk migrates from defamation to consumer law.

## New Zealand

Home jurisdiction. Treat it as the default.

### Defamation Act 1992

- **Truth (s.8).** Complete defence where the imputation is true, or not
  materially different from the truth. The burden is on us, which is the whole
  reason the source ledgers exist.
- **Honest opinion (ss.9–10).** Protects genuinely held opinion. Where the
  publisher is not the author, the publisher must believe the opinion was the
  author's genuine opinion. As everywhere, the opinion must rest on facts that
  are true — which is why the first-person second sentence must sit next to a
  sourced first sentence.
- **Bodies corporate (s.6).** A company may bring defamation proceedings only
  if it alleges and proves that the publication has caused, or is likely to
  cause, **pecuniary loss**. This is a real filter for a small publisher
  criticising a large company, and it is the closest NZ equivalent to the UK's
  serious-harm threshold.
- **No serious-harm element.** Unlike the UK since 2013 and Australia since
  2021, NZ has no general statutory seriousness filter for individuals. The
  practical bar to starting a claim is therefore *lower* here than in the UK.
  Do not assume UK-style protection applies at home.
- **Limitation** is short — two years, subject to the court's power to extend.
  Materially republishing or substantially editing a page can restart the clock,
  which matters for a series that re-imports and re-publishes content.

### Public interest — *Durie v Gardiner* [2018] NZCA 278

The Court of Appeal established a public interest defence for responsible
communication on matters of public interest. Two limbs: the subject matter must
be of public interest, and the communication must be responsible.

**Responsibility is judged on process** — seriousness of the allegation, steps
taken to verify, whether the other side's position was sought and included.

That is, almost exactly, the `rtm-exit-guide` research protocol. A page built to
that protocol, with a ledger behind it, *is* the evidence for this defence. It
is also why the ledgers deliberately record fairness context — for example
YouTube's own statement that most users are never asked for ID, and Amazon's
denial that anything is proven. **Keep including the company's side.** It costs
a clause and it is the difference between a strong page and a reckless one.

### Fair Trading Act 1986 — the one that actually bites

Once this platform is "in trade" — selling courses, taking subscriptions,
earning affiliate commission — the FTA applies to what it says.

- **s.9: misleading or deceptive conduct in trade.** Broad, strict, and it does
  not require intent. An overstated claim about an alternative we earn from is
  squarely in range.
- Enforced by the **Commerce Commission**, which prosecutes and publicises.
  Penalties for a body corporate run into the hundreds of thousands per offence.
- Unlike defamation, **the injured party is the reader, not the company.** A
  complaint can come from anyone.

For a platform whose asset is trust, an FTA finding is worse than a defamation
letter: it says we misled the people we claim to be helping.

### Also relevant

- **Advertising Standards Authority (NZ).** Self-regulatory. The Advertising
  Standards Code requires ads to be identifiable as such, which reaches
  affiliate content. Rulings are published — reputational, not financial.
- **Harmful Digital Communications Act 2015.** Aimed at communications causing
  serious emotional distress to *individuals*. Not a corporate tool. It becomes
  live only if a page targets a named person, which the house rules already
  forbid.

## Australia

Counter-intuitively the safest of the five for criticising a large company, and
one of the sharper ones for how we sell.

### Uniform Defamation Acts (state-based; e.g. Defamation Act 2005 (NSW))

Amended by the Model Defamation Amendment Provisions, in force in most states
from 1 July 2021.

- **Corporations generally cannot sue (s.9).** A corporation has no cause of
  action unless it is an "excluded corporation" — broadly, a not-for-profit, or
  one with **fewer than 10 employees** that is not related to another
  corporation. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Netflix, Uber and PayPal are
  all comfortably outside that. **They cannot sue us for defamation in
  Australia.**
- **Serious harm (s.10A, 2021).** Now an element of the cause of action, and it
  can be determined early rather than at trial.
- **Public interest defence (s.29A, 2021).** Modelled on the UK's s.4.
- **Single publication rule (2021).** Limitation — one year — runs from first
  publication rather than resetting on each download.

### Injurious falsehood — the route a corporation actually has

Because s.9 closes defamation to large companies, a corporate complainant in
Australia uses injurious falsehood. It requires:

1. A false statement about the claimant's goods or business, **and**
2. **Malice** — knowledge of falsity or reckless indifference to truth, **and**
3. Actual economic damage

The malice element is the protection, and it maps directly onto our process.
Publishing a verified claim from a named source is close to the opposite of
recklessness. Publishing an unverified statistic attributed to an institution
that never said it is what recklessness looks like from the outside — which is
precisely why the "92% of Western data" line was cut rather than softened.

### Australian Consumer Law (Competition and Consumer Act 2010, Sch 2)

- **s.18: misleading or deceptive conduct** in trade or commerce. No intent
  required. The analogue of NZ's FTA s.9.
- **s.29: false or misleading representations** about goods or services,
  including testimonials and endorsements.
- Enforced by the **ACCC**, which has an active interest in influencer and
  affiliate disclosure.

### *Fairfax Media v Voller* [2021] HCA 27 — watch the comments

The High Court held that a page operator can be a **publisher of third-party
comments** on its own social posts. If this platform runs comments, or posts to
social where replies accumulate under our own content, we may carry
responsibility for what other people write there about a named company.

Practical consequence: if a TikTok or Mastodon post about a named company
attracts a comment alleging something we would never print ourselves, that
comment is a risk we may own. Moderate it.

## United Kingdom, European Union, United States

Covered in depth in `rtm-leave-validation/references/legal.md`. In brief:

- **UK.** Defamation Act 2013: serious harm (s.1), truth (s.2), honest opinion
  (s.3 — **must indicate the basis of the opinion**), public interest (s.4).
  The s.3 requirement is the strongest legal argument for publishing sources on
  the page rather than keeping them in a private ledger.
- **EU.** Member-state defamation law, some of it criminal. Comparative
  advertising governed by Directive 2006/114/EC — comparisons must be objective
  and verifiable. Misleading *omissions* are actionable under 2005/29/EC, which
  is a second reason the mandatory catch disclosure exists.
- **US.** Plaintiff proves falsity; large companies usually public figures
  needing actual malice; opinion disclosing its basis is protected. The realistic
  exposure is not defamation but the **FTC Endorsement Guides** and, if we ever
  compete commercially with something we review, the Lanham Act.

## What this means in practice

1. **Write for New Zealand and the UK.** They are the strictest for what we say.
   Copy that survives both survives Australia and the US comfortably.
2. **Behave for Australia and the US.** They are the strictest for how we sell.
   Disclosure discipline is set by the ACCC and the FTC, not by NZ practice.
3. **Check the EU only when a page targets a member state** — if a guide is
   written for, say, German readers, German law is the one that matters, and
   some states retain criminal defamation.
4. **The company's own words are the strongest material in every market at
   once.** A quoted privacy policy is true, provable, non-malicious, in the
   public interest, and not comparative advertising. When a claim can be made
   from a company's own documents, make it that way.
