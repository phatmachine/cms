This supersedes the earlier review of the Memories-and-cost draft. That draft's lead claim (that Snap deletes your newest Memories) was contradicted by Snap's own help page and was removed with the angle.

## Verdict
Every factual claim on the rewritten page traces to a primary source or a named outlet, and I re-read the sources before writing it. The page is safe on facts, but it is a youth-safety guide naming a company in live litigation and under a regulator's order. Under the defensible-content rules that means a lawyer reads reasons 02 and 03 and the standfirst before it goes live. The page stays `draft` for that reason and for the missing media.

Written by the same session that researched it. Where a ledger row rests on a search summary rather than a page I read, the ledger says so.

## Legal
**The word "evil".** Not printed. A moral epithet about a company is an imputation of motive that cannot be evidenced, and it is the construction most likely to turn a sourced page into an opinion piece that can be dismissed. The editor's view is carried in first person, directly after the facts it rests on: "I hate being asked to parent from a list of names", "I hate what they describe". That is recognisable opinion, with its basis visible, on facts that are true. It is as strong as the page can safely be.

**Reason 02 (FTC).** A regulator's own charge, dated and attributed, is the safest kind of sentence. The risk is the verb: "charged" and "settled" are accurate; "found" would not be. The 2025 sentence ends "a referral is not a finding", which pre-empts the overreach. Holds in all five markets.

**Reason 03 and the standfirst (litigation).** Allegations in live cases against a litigious company, in a child-safety context, which the skill lists as a reason to get counsel. The page says "allegations, not findings", attributes the settlement to Snap's own filing ("on confidential terms") and avoids "admitted" or "liable". Exposure is lowest in Australia and the US; New Mexico and Nevada are US public-record proceedings. For the UK and New Zealand a lawyer should still read it, because the reader may act on it (leave an app). **Needs a human: yes.**

**Fairness.** Snap disputes the New Mexico claims and says it cares about safety. The page does not print that, and the "allegations, not findings" line does the work. A lawyer may prefer one clause of Snap's position.

**Alternatives.** No promise of safety is made in the guide's own voice. Kinzoo is described by what a parent controls, not by outcomes. No affiliate relationship exists: both referral links are plain URLs. If either becomes a commissioned link, disclosure goes beside the recommendation.

**Child-safety rule.** Nothing on the page describes grooming or exploitation methods. The most specific language is the New Mexico complaint's, attributed and left in a source line only.
